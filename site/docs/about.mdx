---
title: What it's for
description: Why a badge is a signed credential rather than a row in your database.
---

A checkmark usually means a row somewhere says so. That works until you need
it to mean something off your server: in another app, on a device with no
signal, or to somebody who doesn't trust your database.

gossmark makes a badge a credential: signed bytes that carry their own proof.

## Why it checks offline

A verifier takes three things and nothing else: the root keys it trusts, a
revocation snapshot, and the moment to check against.

It never opens a connection and never calls a clock. So the same badge,
checked on a plane, gives the same answer as it does online, and two clients
with different wall clocks can't disagree about validity by accident.

The cost is that you must hand it a snapshot. That's deliberate: freshness
becomes something your app decides and can show, instead of something the
library hides.

## Why organizations issue through a capability

A newspaper should be able to badge its own journalists without your team
approving each one. Giving the newspaper a root key would let it badge
anything.

So it gets a capability: permission to issue for a scope. The scope is
checked during verification, not trusted, so an organization approved for one
category can't issue for another.

Revoking that one capability refuses every badge that flowed from it at once.

## Why the subject is not a name

Names change. People rename themselves, organizations rebrand, handles get
transferred.

A credential's subject is a wallet-bound identifier, so none of that touches
the badge. Your product maps identifier to display name, and that mapping is
yours to change.
